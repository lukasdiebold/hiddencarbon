Final Project Presentation
Hi everyone. Our project is called Hidden Carbon. It's a browser extension that estimates the carbon cost of using LLM chat assistants — Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini — and puts that number in terms you already have a feel for. I'm Lukas, this is Hari, and I'll walk you through the motivation, how it works, and what we found.

So here's the problem we started with. When you send a message to an LLM, it feels exactly like sending a text. It replies in a second and you move on. There's no price on the message and no meter running, so you have no way to tell whether asking one more question is more like doing a web search or more like streaming a video. Two things make this worth caring about. First, the scale: OpenAI reported around two and a half billion ChatGPT prompts a day in 2025. And second, the numbers people quote are all over the place — everything from "a single query boils a kettle" to "0.2 grams per search" — and most of them trace back to outdated or contested sources. Honestly, the two of us started using these tools every day, suspected it was worse for the environment than the habits it was replacing, and realized we couldn't answer that even to within a factor of ten. That's what got us started.
2 · Related work
Data-transfer tools

Score an LLM query as ~free — they only see kilobytes, not the GPU.

AI-specific counters

ChatGPT only — and a bare number with nothing to compare it to.

A counter for AI chat — compared against a search and a minute of streaming
We looked at what already exists, and it falls into two groups, neither of which answers our question. The first group estimates emissions from how much data a page transfers. That works fine for a normal webpage, but an LLM query is only a few kilobytes on the wire while the real cost is the GPU work in the data center — so these tools score it as basically free. The second group is built for AI, but they only cover ChatGPT, and they just show you a bare number of grams with nothing to compare it against. So our contribution is a tool scoped specifically to AI chat, across all three major assistants, that reports each conversation's footprint relative to a web search and a minute of streaming — two things you already understand. One thing we deliberately did not do is try to be a carbon meter for your whole browser. That would mean modelling every device, network, and idle server, and the answer would still be uncertain.
3 · System

A Chrome extension, measured per conversation

→
Store
→
Show

Reads the conversation from Claude, ChatGPT & Gemini, stores it per conversation, shows the footprint in the toolbar.

TypeScript · Chrome MV3 · no backend, no telemetry.

So this is what we actually built — a Chrome extension. It works per conversation. There are three small content scripts, one per service. For Claude and ChatGPT we wrap the browser's fetch call and read the conversation straight out of the JSON the site already loads. Gemini doesn't expose that cleanly, so for Gemini we read the conversation from the rendered page instead. A background worker stores the stats for each conversation, so if you close the browser and come back, the footprint is still there. And the toolbar popup, on the right, shows you the emissions, the energy, the token counts, and the equivalent number of searches and minutes of streaming. It's built with TypeScript on Chrome's extension framework, there's no backend and no tracking, and the only network call it makes is once a day to refresh grid data.

Constants calibrated to Epoch AI's ≈0.3 Wh per typical query.[9] Output tokens cost ~6× input — the model writes one token at a time.

Now how do we actually get to a number. It's three steps. First, we count the characters in the conversation and convert them to tokens, using the standard rule of about four characters per token — that's good to roughly fifteen percent for English. Second, we turn tokens into energy. Notice the output tokens cost about six times more than the input tokens, and that's real: the model reads your whole prompt in parallel, but it has to generate the answer one token at a time. Third, we multiply energy by the carbon intensity of the grid to get grams of CO2, and that intensity is the interesting part — I'll get to it on the next slide. We didn't invent these energy constants; we calibrated them against Epoch AI's measurement of about a third of a watt-hour for a typical query. And the fifteen percent error in token counting really doesn't matter, because the energy factors themselves are uncertain by tens of percent.
3 · Methods

Closer data centers count more

Live grid intensity per region from Electricity Maps, averaged with weight ∝ 1 / distance — the region you're most likely routed to dominates.

This is the part we're most proud of, and it's where we differ from the existing tools, which all just use one global average. The problem is we can't see which data center handled your request. But we do know which cloud each service runs on and which US regions are realistic candidates — those are in the table. So for each of those regions we pull the live carbon intensity from Electricity Maps, and we take a weighted average where closer data centers count more, weighted by one over the distance. The reasoning is that providers route you to a nearby region for speed, so the closest one is the most likely. To give you a sense of why this matters: if you're in Seattle, you're far more likely to be served from Oregon, which is 280 kilometers away, than from Virginia, which is 3,700. When we ran this for Claude in May, the weighted intensity came out around 231 grams per kilowatt-hour. Everything's cached and refreshed once a day, and if we have no data we fall back to a global average.

Why we draw the boundary differently

AI chat & search — the work is in the data center
server-side energy
Video streaming — the work is mostly on your screen[3]
device · 72%
network · 23%
data center is only 5% →

So we count server-side for AI, but the whole chain for streaming. Counting just the server for video would report 5% of its real footprint.

I want to be clear about what's inside our number and what isn't, because this is really the heart of the project. We count only the text you typed and the text the model wrote back in this conversation. We leave out the prefill — the system prompt, the tools, the history that gets resent every turn — partly because it's mostly cached on the server, and partly because counting it would make every query look identical. We also leave out training, which we'll come back to, and the network and your device, which are tiny for text. Now, the one thing that looks inconsistent: we draw the boundary differently for streaming. For AI and search we count server-side energy, because that's where the work happens. But for streaming we count the whole chain, because the IEA shows that about 72 percent of streaming energy is in your screen, not the data center. If we only counted the server side there, we'd report five percent of what's really going on. The rule we follow is: count the energy each activity actually causes and that grows as you do more of it. The honest caveat is that this means the streaming comparison is a bit apples-to-oranges, so treat it as rough scale, not a precise ratio.
3 · Methods · baselines

The numbers people quote are wrong

a web search[2]
0.2 g → 0.02 g / query
a minute of streaming[3]
6 kg / hr → 0.6 g / min

Both popular figures had errors or were decades out of date. We took each baseline from the corrected primary source.

Our two baselines are a web search and a minute of streaming, and getting these right took some digging, because the popular numbers are wrong. For search we use 0.02 grams. That comes from Vanderbauwhede, who took Google's old 2009 figure and updated it for better hardware, a more efficient data center, and today's grid. It lands about ten times below the 0.2 grams that people still cite. And note this is plain search — the AI Overviews Google now shows run sixty to seventy-five times higher, but we can't tell when those fire. For streaming we use 0.6 grams a minute, which is the IEA's figure after they corrected a widely shared claim that had streaming at six kilograms an hour — that one had an actual unit-conversion error in it. So the pattern we kept running into is that the numbers in circulation are inflated or out of date, and that's why we went back to the original papers for every baseline.
4 · Results

Where one LLM query lands

≈ 7 searches, and a fraction of a minute of video.

Not negligible — but nowhere near the "kettle of water" you read about. (500 in / 1,000 out tokens; Claude grid ≈231 g/kWh.)

So here's the answer to the question we started with. Take a representative Claude turn — 500 tokens in, a thousand out — at the grid intensity we computed. That works out to about two-thirds of a watt-hour, and about 0.15 grams of CO2. In familiar terms, that's roughly seven web searches, or about a quarter-minute of streaming. Please don't read too much into the exact digits — the point is where it lands. An LLM message costs noticeably more than a single search, on the order of a handful of them. But a long video session still outweighs a whole afternoon of chatting. Putting the number in that range, between two things you already understand, is exactly what we set out to do. It's not nothing, but it's also not the kettle of water you read about.
5 · Discussion · cross-check

A money-based estimate is 7× higher

Ours (physics)
0.15 g
Spend-based[4]
1.06 g

And we can't tell which way the error runs.

Margins would push it high; pricing inference below cost pushes it low. The gap really shows that price has come loose from physical cost in this market.

To check ourselves, we tried a completely different method. Instead of physics, this one works from money: you take what you'd pay for the API and multiply by an EPA emission factor for data hosting. That gives 1.06 grams, where our bottom-up number was 0.15 — about seven times apart. What's interesting is that we can't even say which one is wrong. Normally a spend-based number runs high, because the price includes profit margin that doesn't buy any electricity. But right now the AI companies are pricing inference below cost, subsidized by investors, which would push it the other way and make it run low. So the most useful way to read this gap is that in this market the price has come loose from the actual physical cost. We only use this as a sanity check, never as our headline number — partly because it also assigns basically zero to free services like search and YouTube, which is obviously wrong.
5 · Discussion · training

"But what about training?"

use-phase only
0.15 g
+ amortized training (industry inference : training ratio[6][7])
use
training

≈ 1.7–2× → about 0.25–0.3 g over the model's life.

For popular models, training roughly doubles the number — it doesn't change the story. (For rarely-used models it can dominate.)

The obvious objection at this point is: you're ignoring training. So we checked. The naive way is to take one model's training footprint and divide it over all its queries, but that's unreliable — it leaves out all the failed runs and earlier versions, and you're basically guessing the query count. A steadier way is to look at the whole industry's ratio of inference energy to training energy. Patterson and colleagues put Google at about sixty-forty, which works out to a 1.7 times multiplier on the use-phase number. McKinsey's more recent figures put training and inference at roughly equal today, which is about two times. So call it 1.7 to 2x. Applied to our 0.15 grams, the full lifecycle is something like a quarter to a third of a gram. The takeaway is that for popular models training roughly doubles the number — it doesn't change the story by an order of magnitude. For a model almost nobody uses, though, training can dominate.

Energy factors

Multimodal

Narrow scope, constants from primary literature, boundary stated out loud — an estimate we can defend, not one that looks more precise than it is.

I want to be upfront about where this falls short. Reasoning models cost roughly ten times more per output token, and we don't tell them apart yet. The streaming comparison mixes the two boundaries I mentioned. We infer which data center serves you rather than observing it. We approximate tokens with the characters-over-four rule. The underlying energy factors are uncertain by tens of percent. And we don't handle images or audio yet. That said, the way we worked the whole time was to keep the scope narrow, take our constants from the published literature, and state the boundary out loud. We'd rather give you a number we can actually defend than one that looks precise and isn't.
6 · Conclusion
An LLM message ≈ a handful of searches.
A live meter across Claude, ChatGPT & Gemini — measured against things you already understand. Not a guilt trip; just the number, so you can decide for yourself.
To wrap up. We built a working extension across Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini that shows you a CO2 estimate for each conversation as you go. It expresses that estimate against two familiar baselines, web searches and minutes of streaming, both sourced from corrected primary figures. The main finding is that an LLM message costs about a handful of searches — not negligible, but well below the alarming numbers you usually hear. And along the way we found a second thing: in this market, price has drifted away from physical cost, and that's exactly what breaks the spend-based accounting method. The goal here was never to make anyone feel guilty — it's a quiet meter that lets you see the cost and decide for yourself. Thanks — we're happy to take questions.
